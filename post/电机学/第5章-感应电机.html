§ Induction Motor Rotor Construction

Induction motors use a stator that produces a rotating magnetic field and a rotor that relies on electromagnetic induction. Rotor types:

  1. Squirrel-cage rotor: Consists of thick conducting bars laid in slots on the rotor core, short-circuited by end rings. It is simple, rugged, and requires no brushes.
  2. Wound rotor: Features a complete three-phase winding connected to slip rings and brushes. External resistors can be added to control starting current and torque.

§ Principle of Operation

As the stator magnetic field rotates, it cuts the rotor bars and induces a voltage:

eind=(v⃗×B⃗)⋅l⃗e_{ind} = (\vec{v} \times \vec{B}) \cdot \vec{l} eind​=(v×B)⋅l

where v⃗\vec{v}v is the velocity of the bar relative to the magnetic field. The induced voltage drives a rotor current. Because of the rotor's leakage inductance, the rotor current lags behind the induced voltage. The interaction of the rotor current with the stator field produces torque:

τ⃗ind=kB⃗R×B⃗S\vec{\tau}_{ind} = k \vec{B}_R \times \vec{B}_S τind​=kBR​×BS​

This torque accelerates the rotor in the direction of the rotating field.

§ Torque-Speed Characteristics of an Induction Motor

The induced torque is:

Neglecting stator impedance:

The torque-speed curve is divided into:

  1. Low-slip region: s→0s \to 0s→0, R2/s≫X2R_2/s \gg X_2R2​/s≫X2​, rotor power factor ≈1\approx 1≈1, torque is linear with slip.
  2. Moderate-slip region: Rotor reactance sX2s X_2sX2​ becomes comparable to R2R_2R2​. Torque reaches a maximum (pullout torque).
  3. High-slip region: s→1s \to 1s→1. High current but low torque due to low power factor.

§ Maximum (Pullout) Torque

By impedance matching, maximum torque occurs at:

smax=R2′RTH2+(XTH+X2′)2s_{max} = \frac{R_2'}{\sqrt{R_{TH}^2 + (X_{TH} + X_2')^2}} smax​=RTH2​+(XTH​+X2′​)2​R2′​​

The maximum torque is independent of rotor resistance R2R_2R2​, but the slip at which it occurs is proportional to R2R_2R2​.

§ Control by Rotor Bar Structure and NEMA Classes

Wound-rotor motors can vary R2R_2R2​ using external resistors. For cage rotors, variable rotor resistance is achieved using:

  • Deep rotor bars: At starting, high rotor frequency (equal to stator frequency) causes current to crowd to the top of the bars (skin effect), increasing resistance. At running, low rotor frequency allows current to distribute evenly, reducing resistance.
  • Double-cage rotor: Has an outer cage of high resistance/low reactance and an inner cage of low resistance/high reactance.

NEMA Design Classes (A, B, C, D) represent different starting torque and current characteristics.

§ Speed Control of an Induction Motor

Methods for speed control:

  1. Changing line voltage: Torque is proportional to V2V^2V2. High slip operation results in high rotor losses and low efficiency. Limited speed range.
  2. Changing rotor resistance: Wound-rotor only. Simple but inefficient.
  3. Variable Voltage Variable Frequency (VVVF) control: To prevent saturation, the stator voltage is scaled with frequency (V∝fV \propto fV∝f). Above rated frequency, voltage is constant and torque limit decreases (τ∝1/f\tau \propto 1/fτ∝1/f).

§ Starting of Induction Motors

The starting current is very large (5 to 8 times rated) while the starting torque is modest. Methods to reduce starting current:

  • Reduced-voltage starting (using autotransformer or star-delta starter).
  • Deep rotor bars / double cage.
  • Rotor resistance (wound rotor).
