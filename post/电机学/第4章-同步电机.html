§ Synchronous Generator Construction

Synchronous generators (also called alternators) convert mechanical power into three-phase electrical power. Their construction consists of two main parts:

  • Field windings: Located on the rotor, supplied with DC current to produce the main magnetic field.
  • Armature windings: Located on the stator, where three-phase AC voltages are induced.

There are two rotor types:

  1. Salient-pole rotor: Large diameter and short axial length, with poles projecting outward from the rotor core. Used in low-speed applications (such as hydro-turbines).
  2. Non-salient pole (cylindrical) rotor: Small diameter and long axial length, with field windings embedded in slots. Used in high-speed applications (such as steam or gas turbines).

The speed of rotation of a synchronous generator nmn_mnm​ (in RPM) is directly locked to the electrical frequency fef_efe​:

fe=Pnm120f_e = \frac{P n_m}{120} fe​=120Pnm​​

where PPP is the number of poles.

§ Armature Reaction

When a synchronous generator operates under no-load conditions, the rotor field current IfI_fIf​ produces a rotor magnetic field B0B_0B0​ (associated with field MMF FfF_fFf​), which rotates at synchronous speed and induces a three-phase voltage in the stator windings.

Under load, three-phase currents flow through the stator windings, producing their own rotating stator magnetic field BaB_aBa​ (associated with armature MMF FaF_aFa​). The distortion of the net air-gap magnetic field by stator currents is called armature reaction.

§ Equivalent Circuit of a Synchronous Generator

To model the generator electrically under load, we assume linear magnetic conditions (neglecting saturation). Under this assumption, the net air-gap flux density BnetB_{net}Bnet​ is the vector sum of the rotor field BfB_fBf​ and the armature reaction field BaB_aBa​:

B⃗net=B⃗f+B⃗a\vec{B}_{net} = \vec{B}_f + \vec{B}_a Bnet​=Bf​+Ba​

Correspondingly, the net induced voltage E⃗s\vec{E}_sEs​ in the stator is:

E⃗s=E⃗0+E⃗a\vec{E}_s = \vec{E}_0 + \vec{E}_a Es​=E0​+Ea​

Since the armature reaction field B⃗a\vec{B}_aBa​ is produced by the armature current I⃗\vec{I}I, the induced EMF E⃗a\vec{E}_aEa​ lags the armature current I⃗\vec{I}I by 90∘90^\circ90∘. We can represent this relationship using an armature reaction reactance XaX_aXa​:

E⃗a=−jI⃗Xa\vec{E}_a = -j \vec{I} X_a Ea​=−jIXa​

Including the stator armature leakage reactance XlX_lXl​ and winding resistance RAR_ARA​, the terminal voltage V⃗ϕ\vec{V}_\phiVϕ​ is:

V⃗ϕ=E⃗s−jI⃗Xl−I⃗RA=E⃗0−jI⃗Xa−jI⃗Xl−I⃗RA\vec{V}_\phi = \vec{E}_s - j\vec{I}X_l - \vec{I}R_A = \vec{E}_0 - j\vec{I}X_a - j\vec{I}X_l - \vec{I}R_A Vϕ​=Es​−jIXl​−IRA​=E0​−jIXa​−jIXl​−IRA​

We define the total synchronous reactance as Xs=Xa+XlX_s = X_a + X_lXs​=Xa​+Xl​. Thus:

E⃗0=V⃗ϕ+I⃗RA+jI⃗Xs\vec{E}_0 = \vec{V}_\phi + \vec{I}R_A + j\vec{I}X_s E0​=Vϕ​+IRA​+jIXs​

The per-phase equivalent circuit of a synchronous generator consists of:

  1. Rotor field circuit (DC): Excitation voltage VFV_FVF​ supplying the field winding RFR_FRF​ and adjustable resistance RadjR_{adj}Radj​.
  2. Stator armature circuit (AC): The internal generated voltage E0E_0E0​ in series with RAR_ARA​ and jXsjX_sjXs​ supplying the phase terminal voltage VϕV_\phiVϕ​.

§ Phasor Diagram and Armature Reaction States

The angle ψ\psiψ is defined as the phase angle between the stator current I⃗\vec{I}I and the excitation EMF E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​. This angle determines the nature of the armature reaction:

  1. Cross-magnetizing armature reaction (ψ=0∘\psi = 0^\circψ=0∘): The stator current I⃗\vec{I}I is in phase with E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​. The armature MMF F⃗a\vec{F}_aFa​ lies along the q-axis, perpendicular to the field MMF F⃗f\vec{F}_fFf​ (d-axis). It distorts the field but does not demagnetize or magnetize it.
  2. Demagnetizing armature reaction (ψ=90∘\psi = 90^\circψ=90∘): The stator current I⃗\vec{I}I lags E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​ by 90∘90^\circ90∘ (purely inductive load). The armature MMF F⃗a\vec{F}_aFa​ directly opposes the field MMF F⃗f\vec{F}_fFf​ along the d-axis, reducing the net flux.
  3. Magnetizing armature reaction (ψ=−90∘\psi = -90^\circψ=−90∘): The stator current I⃗\vec{I}I leads E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​ by 90∘90^\circ90∘ (purely capacitive load). The armature MMF F⃗a\vec{F}_aFa​ aids the field MMF F⃗f\vec{F}_fFf​ along the d-axis, increasing the net flux.

§ Salient-Pole Machines and Two-Reaction Theory

In salient-pole machines, the air-gap length is highly non-uniform. The reluctance along the pole axis (d-axis) is much smaller than the reluctance along the interpolar axis (q-axis). Therefore, the armature reaction cannot be described by a single synchronous reactance XsX_sXs​.

Using two-reaction theory, we resolve the armature MMF F⃗a\vec{F}_aFa​ and current I⃗\vec{I}I into direct-axis (d-axis) and quadrature-axis (q-axis) components:

I⃗=I⃗d+I⃗q\vec{I} = \vec{I}_d + \vec{I}_q I=Id​+Iq​

Id=Isin⁡ψ,Iq=Icos⁡ψI_d = I \sin\psi, \quad I_q = I \cos\psi Id​=Isinψ,Iq​=Icosψ

The corresponding reactances are the d-axis synchronous reactance XdX_dXd​ and q-axis synchronous reactance XqX_qXq​:

Xd=Xl+XadX_d = X_l + X_{ad} Xd​=Xl​+Xad​

Xq=Xl+XaqX_q = X_l + X_{aq} Xq​=Xl​+Xaq​

Since the d-axis reluctance is smaller, the d-axis permeance is higher, meaning:

Xad>Xaq  ⟹  Xd>XqX_{ad} > X_{aq} \implies X_d > X_q Xad​>Xaq​⟹Xd​>Xq​

The excitation EMF E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​ is given by:

E⃗0=V⃗ϕ+I⃗RA+jI⃗dXd+jI⃗qXq\vec{E}_0 = \vec{V}_\phi + \vec{I}R_A + j\vec{I}_d X_d + j\vec{I}_q X_q E0​=Vϕ​+IRA​+jId​Xd​+jIq​Xq​

To draw the phasor diagram, we locate the d-axis and q-axis. The power angle δ\deltaδ is the angle between E⃗0\vec{E}_0E0​ and V⃗ϕ\vec{V}_\phiVϕ​, and ψ=δ+ϕ\psi = \delta + \phiψ=δ+ϕ, where ϕ\phiϕ is the power factor angle of the load.

§ Open-Circuit and Short-Circuit Tests

The parameters RAR_ARA​ and XsX_sXs​ of a synchronous generator can be determined by two tests:

  1. Open-Circuit Test (OCC): The generator is driven at rated speed with open armature terminals. The field current IfI_fIf​ is varied, and the terminal voltage Voc=E0V_{oc} = E_0Voc​=E0​ is measured.
  2. Short-Circuit Test (SCC): The armature terminals are short-circuited. The field current IfI_fIf​ is increased from zero, and the armature current Isc=IAI_{sc} = I_AIsc​=IA​ is measured.
  • Why the SCC is a straight line: Under short-circuit, the impedance is dominated by the synchronous reactance (Xs≫RAX_s \gg R_AXs​≫RA​). The load is almost purely inductive (ψ≈90∘\psi \approx 90^\circψ≈90∘). The armature reaction is strongly demagnetizing, meaning the net MMF Fnet=Ff−FaF_{net} = F_f - F_aFnet​=Ff​−Fa​ is very small, keeping the machine far from saturation.

The synchronous impedance ZsZ_sZs​ is calculated at a given field current:

Zs=RA2+Xs2=E0 (from OCC)Isc (from SCC)Z_s = \sqrt{R_A^2 + X_s^2} = \frac{E_0 \text{ (from OCC)}}{I_{sc} \text{ (from SCC)}} Zs​=RA2​+Xs2​​=Isc​ (from SCC)E0​ (from OCC)​

Since Xs≫RAX_s \gg R_AXs​≫RA​, we have Xs≈ZsX_s \approx Z_sXs​≈Zs​.

The Short-Circuit Ratio (SCR) is defined as:

SCR=If,oc(VN)If,sc(IN)\text{SCR} = \frac{I_{f,oc} (V_N)}{I_{f,sc} (I_N)} SCR=If,sc​(IN​)If,oc​(VN​)​

SCR is the reciprocal of the per-unit synchronous reactance:

SCR=1Xs,pu\text{SCR} = \frac{1}{X_{s,pu}} SCR=Xs,pu​1​

§ Slip Test for Measuring Xd and Xq

In salient-pole machines, XdX_dXd​ and XqX_qXq​ are measured using a slip test:

  1. The field winding is open-circuited (If=0I_f = 0If​=0).
  2. A balanced three-phase voltage at reduced magnitude is applied to the armature windings.
  3. The rotor is driven by a prime mover at a speed slightly different from the synchronous speed (a very small slip).

As the stator MMF slowly slips past the rotor poles:

  • When the stator MMF aligns with the rotor d-axis, the reluctance is minimum, flux is maximum, current is minimum (IminI_{min}Imin​), and voltage is maximum (VmaxV_{max}Vmax​).
  • When the stator MMF aligns with the rotor q-axis, the reluctance is maximum, flux is minimum, current is maximum (ImaxI_{max}Imax​), and voltage is minimum (VminV_{min}Vmin​).

The reactances are calculated as:

Xd=Vmax3IminX_d = \frac{V_{max}}{\sqrt{3} I_{min}} Xd​=3​Imin​Vmax​​

Xq=Vmin3ImaxX_q = \frac{V_{min}}{\sqrt{3} I_{max}} Xq​=3​Imax​Vmin​​

§ Zero-Power-Factor Test and Potier Triangle

To determine the leakage reactance XlX_lXl​ and separate it from the armature reaction, a zero-power-factor (ZPF) test is conducted:

  1. The generator is loaded with an adjustable purely inductive load (power factor cos⁡ϕ=0\cos\phi = 0cosϕ=0 lagging).
  2. The field current IfI_fIf​ is adjusted while maintaining the armature current at its rated value INI_NIN​.
  3. The terminal voltage VVV is recorded for various values of IfI_fIf​. This yields the ZPF characteristic.

Using the Potier Triangle, we can determine the Potier reactance XpX_pXp​ (which is close to XlX_lXl​) and the demagnetizing armature reaction MMF FaF_aFa​.

§ Voltage Regulation of a Synchronous Generator

Voltage regulation (VR) is the percentage change in terminal voltage when rated load is removed:

VR=E0−VV×100%\text{VR} = \frac{E_0 - V}{V} \times 100\% VR=VE0​−V​×100%

  • Lagging PF (inductive load): I⃗\vec{I}I lags V⃗ϕ\vec{V}_\phiVϕ​. The armature reaction is demagnetizing, causing terminal voltage to drop sharply as load increases. VR is positive and large.
  • Unity PF (resistive load): I⃗\vec{I}I is in phase with V⃗ϕ\vec{V}_\phiVϕ​. Terminal voltage drops slightly. VR is positive and small.
  • Leading PF (capacitive load): I⃗\vec{I}I leads V⃗ϕ\vec{V}_\phiVϕ​. The armature reaction is magnetizing, causing terminal voltage to rise under load. VR can be negative.

§ External and Regulation Characteristics

The relationship between terminal voltage and load current (VTV_TVT​ vs IAI_AIA​) is the external characteristic. The relationship between field current and load current (IfI_fIf​ vs IAI_AIA​) required to keep terminal voltage constant is the regulation characteristic.

§ Frequency-Power Characteristics of a Generator

The mechanical speed of a generator's prime mover is inversely proportional to the load power:

SD=nnl−nflnfl×100%\text{SD} = \frac{n_{nl} - n_{fl}}{n_{fl}} \times 100\% SD=nfl​nnl​−nfl​​×100%

The frequency-power relation is modeled as:

P=sp(fnl−fsys)P = s_p (f_{nl} - f_{sys}) P=sp​(fnl​−fsys​)

where:

  • fnlf_{nl}fnl​: no-load frequency of the generator
  • fsysf_{sys}fsys​: system frequency
  • sps_psp​: governor slope (kW/Hz)

If a generator's active power output PPP is kept constant:

§ Parallel Operation of AC Generators

To connect a generator in parallel with an existing system, four conditions must be met:

  1. Voltage magnitudes: The RMS line voltages of the oncoming generator and the running system must be equal.
  2. Phase sequence: Both systems must have the same phase sequence (e.g., a−b−ca-b-ca−b−c).
  3. Phase angles: The phase angles of the two systems must be identical.
  4. Frequency: The frequency of the oncoming generator must be slightly higher than the system frequency to prevent it from acting as a motor immediately upon connection.

§ Parallel Operation with an Infinite Bus

When a generator is connected to an infinite bus:

  • The system frequency fsysf_{sys}fsys​ and terminal voltage VTV_TVT​ are fixed.
  • Adjusting the governor shifts the no-load frequency fnlf_{nl}fnl​, changing the active power output PPP of the generator:

    PG=sp(fnl−fsys)P_G = s_p (f_{nl} - f_{sys}) PG​=sp​(fnl​−fsys​)

  • Adjusting the field excitation IfI_fIf​ changes the reactive power output QQQ without affecting active power.

§ Power Sharing between Parallel Generators

When two generators of comparable size operate in parallel, the total active and reactive load must be shared between them:

Pload=PG1+PG2P_{load} = P_{G1} + P_{G2} Pload​=PG1​+PG2​

Qload=QG1+QG2Q_{load} = Q_{G1} + Q_{G2} Qload​=QG1​+QG2​

  • Adjusting the governor of one generator changes the active power allocation between them and alters the system frequency unless both governors are adjusted in opposite directions.
  • Adjusting the excitation of one generator changes the reactive power allocation between them and alters the terminal voltage.

§ Synchronous Generator Transients

When a generator is synchronized to a grid, transients occur due to phase and speed differences:

  • If the generator speed is slightly faster before connection, a positive torque is induced:

    t⃗ind=kB⃗R×B⃗net\vec{t}_{ind} = k \vec{B}_R \times \vec{B}_{net} tind​=kBR​×Bnet​

    This torque acts as a mechanical load on the rotor, slowing it down to match the synchronous speed of the power grid.

§ Synchronous Generator Ratings

Synchronous machine ratings protect the windings and core from thermal and insulation breakdown:

  • Voltage and Frequency: Operating at lower frequencies (e.g. 50Hz for a 60Hz machine) requires reducing the rated voltage by the same proportion to prevent magnetic saturation.
  • Apparent Power: Limited by stator winding heating (IA2RAI_A^2 R_AIA2​RA​).

    Srated=3Vϕ,ratedIA,ratedS_{rated} = 3 V_{\phi,rated} I_{A,rated} Srated​=3Vϕ,rated​IA,rated​

  • Power Factor: Limited by field winding heating (If2RfI_f^2 R_fIf2​Rf​).

§ Synchronous Motor

A synchronous motor has the same physical structure as a generator but converts electrical power to mechanical power. At starting (nm=0n_m = 0nm​=0), the average torque is zero because the magnetic field rotates too quickly for the rotor to accelerate.

Methods to start a synchronous motor:

  1. Assisted start: Use an auxiliary prime mover (pony motor) to accelerate the rotor to synchronous speed before applying excitation.
  2. Variable frequency drive: Supply power at a low frequency and ramp it up as the motor accelerates.
  3. Amortisseur (damper) windings: Short-circuited bars in the pole faces act as an induction motor rotor during starting.
